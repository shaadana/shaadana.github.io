# **Affect in Knowledge Tracing: A Plain-Language Overview**

The [BKT experiment](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1_D0FpDlmloKEWEc5UA-5sR3ZJr8Cz7HYX_xLOMC_QRo) asked whether estimates of students’ emotions could help predict their later answers. Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) keeps a running estimate of what a student knows about each topic. We compared a version using previous answers with one that also used supplied scores for frustration, confusion, concentration, and boredom. These scores adjusted the model’s estimated chance of learning after an answer. Adding affect slightly improved its estimates of how likely later answers were to be correct across all five comparisons, but the gain stayed below the study’s chosen threshold for a meaningful benefit.

The [DKT experiment](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1_Rb2VhF3EPkhXp4S-fbPOjkiuMVX2OxEw6DpGN78IF8) tested the same idea using a neural network that learns patterns across a student’s sequence of answers, including different topics. Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT) also had versions with and without affect. In the affect version, scores from the previous interaction entered the network alongside information about that answer and could change what the network remembered. Adding affect helped in only 3 of 15 comparisons. Its benefit was therefore inconsistent, and none of the comparisons reached the study’s chosen threshold.

[Taken together](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1yfoUv8K9mfVkCtiyel5VBhEsRWfVKpuLM5RXYquWNuc), the experiments found little added predictive value from these particular affect scores. That does not mean emotions are unimportant to learning: the scores were computer-generated estimates, and correct answers were only an indirect sign of mastery. The experiments also cannot show that emotions caused learning. ASSISTments 2012 supported the affect comparisons; ASSISTments 2009 provided an additional check of models without affect because it lacked the necessary emotion scores.
